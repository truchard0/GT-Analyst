# How to make the engine better: research review, 2026-09-20

Scope: what the forecasting-science, LLM-forecasting, market-microstructure, conflict and macro literatures say about this engine's diagnosed weaknesses, checked against fresh measurements of the ledger. Analysis of a forecasting system, not investment advice. Items marked (unverified) rest on a secondary source or on recall and should be checked before they are relied on.

## 1. Bottom line

The engine's main problem is not the doctrine. It is that the book is too small, too correlated and too narrowly priced for its own scoreboard to tell it anything, and that it has no outside reference to be measured against. Three changes matter more than everything else in this review. First, score against a named reference forecast (the market price where one exists, a base rate where one does not) rather than against a coin. Second, make each row start from an explicit outside-view number, and let rows be revised in small steps during their life instead of being stated once. Third, stop forecasting the direction of the next leg in the Iran cluster and forecast levels, durations and structural markers instead, which is what the conflict literature says is forecastable. Most of the rest is plumbing that makes those three cheap.

## 2. What the ledger says when measured properly

These are new numbers from the 47 resolved rows.

The engine's Brier is 0.221 against 0.25 for a coin, but a constant forecast equal to the book's own hit rate (0.745) would have scored 0.190. Against that reference the skill score is negative (-0.16). The reference is hindsight, so it is a harsh test, but it shows where the loss comes from: the engine's mean stated probability is 0.66 while 74.5% of its calls came true. It is underconfident on average and its spread of probabilities adds nothing. Shrinking every stated probability halfway toward the hit rate would have scored 0.2015.

Forty-six of 47 rows were stated above 0.5, and 45 of 47 sit between 0.55 and 0.85. The engine always writes the side it believes, in a band thirty points wide. Resolution is capped by construction: a book that never says 0.15 or 0.93 cannot show much discrimination however good the analysis is.

With the small-sample correction of Ferro and Fricker (2012), reliability falls from 0.041 to 0.027 and resolution from 0.009 to -0.0003, i.e. zero. Their paper shows expected reliability is inflated at least fivefold with fewer than 40 observations per bin; this book has 10 to 24 per bin.

The resolved rows fall in 14 clusters, 21 of them in iran-war. Resampling clusters rather than rows gives a 95% interval for the Brier of 0.158 to 0.256. So the engine cannot yet be distinguished from a coin (the interval reaches 0.25) or from a good forecaster (it reaches 0.16). Iran-war rows score 0.256, which is worse than a coin; everything else scores 0.193. A power calculation from the research (its own arithmetic, not a published figure) puts the requirement for telling 0.22 from 0.25 at roughly 300 independent questions, and the effective sample here is nearer 10 to 15.

Consequence for the 9/19 diagnosis: the "inverted calibration" finding (below 0.65 hits 83% on 24 rows, 0.75 and above hits 60% on 10 rows) is suggestive but not established. Three of five at a true 0.8 happens a quarter of the time by chance. The low-probability bucket being too cautious is the only part with reasonable support. The leg-extrapolation finding stands on different ground: it is a mechanism with outside evidence behind it (section 5), not a bucket statistic.

The ledger stores one probability per row and no revision history, so the engine cannot update and cannot be scored on updating.

## 3. Scoring and statistics

Report the Brier with a cluster-bootstrap interval every time, and treat any bucket or cluster with fewer than about 40 rows as descriptive only. Use the bias-corrected decomposition or drop the decomposition until the book is several times larger. Bradley, Schwartz and Hashino (2008) show the Brier skill score is itself biased low in small samples, so the interval matters more than the point estimate.

Replace "skill versus coin" with skill versus a reference recorded at logging time: the market price when a liquid market exists, otherwise the stated base rate. For each row the edge is (reference - outcome)² minus (forecast - outcome)², and the running mean of that edge, with its cluster interval, is the honest scoreboard. Paleka et al. (2025) make the same recommendation for evaluating LLM forecasters: measure edge over the market, live, across several disjoint windows.

Add the log score beside the Brier. It punishes confident misses harder, which is the failure the high-probability rows are at risk of.

The fastest way to get a usable sample is volume of short, independent, cheaply resolvable rows outside the Iran cluster: scheduled prints, votes, deadlines, filings. Good Judgment forecasters averaged about eight forecasts per question on hundreds of questions a year; this book has 77 rows, all logged between late April and early August, with one forecast each.

## 4. Setting and revising the probability

The best-supported habits in the Good Judgment data are the use of explicit comparison classes and frequent small updates. In Chang et al. (2016), rationales citing a comparison class had the best scores of any principle (0.17 against 0.26 to 1.04 for others, correlational); Karvetski et al. (2022) found comparison-class use and arguing both sides the most robust predictors of accuracy; Atanasov et al. (2020), on 400,000 forecasts, found small frequent updaters most accurate and large infrequent updaters least. A 2025 reanalysis (Hauenstein et al.) finds the headline training and teaming effects shrink or vanish once question selection is controlled, so treat the 6 to 11% training gain as an upper bound. One caution from Good Judgment's own Ukraine post-mortem: leaning on a low base rate without a premortem was how superforecasters missed the invasion.

For this engine that means every row carries three numbers: the base rate with its named reference class (two classes where they disagree, the spread being the uncertainty), the market price if one exists, and the final probability with a one-line reason for the distance from the first two. The rule-check (schema.py) can enforce presence; it cannot enforce quality, which is what the edge score is for.

Do not fit a recalibration curve. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) find even Platt scaling needs a couple of hundred points, and isotonic regression a thousand; Kull et al. (2017) note the logistic family cannot represent "already calibrated" and so can make things worse. Do not extremize either. Baron et al. (2014) and Satopää et al. justify extremizing for averages of forecasters holding different information; for a single forecaster the argument does not apply, and the AIA Forecaster's √3 extremizing corrects a hedging bias measured on thousands of LLM forecasts, not on 47. What the evidence supports at this sample size is a fixed, pre-registered shrink: for example, blend the stated probability with the reference at a set weight, chosen now and scored later. The existing rule that reserves 0.70 and above for invariants and arithmetic-backed prints is a reasonable version of this; the research would add that it should lift the bottom of the range as well, since the under-0.60 rows were the ones most clearly mispriced.

Widen the range honestly. Write rows where the engine believes something is unlikely as low-probability statements of the event itself, rather than flipping every row to the side it favours, and allow above 0.85 where the reference class supports it. That is the only way resolution can become visible.

Add a revisions table (row id, timestamp, probability, reason) and score each row as the time-weighted average of its daily Brier, which is how GJ Open and Metaculus score. This also fixes the "resolved on 7/28, noticed seven weeks later" class of defect, because a daily price check is then part of scoring, not a courtesy.

On premortems and structured techniques: Veinott, Klein and Wiggins (2010) found a premortem cut stated confidence by 25 points against 14 for a pros-and-cons list, but measured confidence only, not accuracy. The one randomized test of Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Dhami, Belton and Mandel, 2019) found no accuracy gain (36% against 33%) and less consistent reasoning. Chang et al. (2018) warn that such techniques assume a bias runs one way and can over-correct (unverified, recalled). The red-team gate has six steps; the evidence supports keeping the premortem and the reference class and would not miss the mini-ACH. The over-correction warning applies directly to the 16.15 leg-neutral gate: a gate that simply flips an escalation bias into a de-escalation bias would repeat the error with the sign changed.

## 5. The Iran cluster: what is forecastable

Fortna (2003) studied 48 interstate ceasefires from 1946 to 1997; war resumed in 21. Resumption hazard was about 27 times higher after a stalemate than after a decisive outcome, and far lower with a demilitarized zone (0.09x), monitors (0.33x) or third-party guarantees; a formal agreement on its own was not significant. (Table figures came through an automated reader; check the PDF before quoting.) Werner and Yuen (2005) find ceasefires produced by outside pressure before the battlefield was clear fail more often, and Beardsley (2008) finds mediated deals hold in the short run and decay as the mediator's leverage fades. A Pakistani- and Qatari-brokered pause in a stalemated war with no monitors is in the worst cell of every one of these tables.

Min (2020), with daily data on every interstate war since 1816, finds that since 1945 negotiations are more frequent and "far less predictive of war termination". A talks round is weak evidence of an ending. That is the 0-for-8 in one sentence.

The ViEWS prediction competitions (Vesco et al. 2022; Hegre et al. 2024) found that at country-month level every model lost to the no-change baseline on the change-focused metric, and that a country's own last twelve months, used as the forecast distribution, is a strong benchmark. What persists is the level and the recent distribution of violence, which includes both legs. What the engine extrapolated was the direction of the last move.

Bargaining theory gives a sorting question. If the obstacle is information about relative strength, fighting reveals it, positions converge and swings damp down (Slantchev 2003). If it is a commitment problem, fighting reveals nothing that helps, pauses are tactical, and the cycle continues until the balance changes or a guarantee appears (Powell 2006, recalled). Enrichment and sanctions sequencing look like the second kind.

Changes that follow: price the next-leg node from the cycle's own history (three fighting rounds and three pauses since February give durations to draw from, few as they are) rather than from the tone of the latest headlines; replace direction rows with duration rows ("pause lasts beyond D"), level rows ("Hormuz transits below X% of pre-war on D") and marker rows ("monitoring mechanism or third-party guarantee announced by D"), since Fortna's markers are the observable things that would justify moving the frozen-conflict probability; and treat a new talks round as close to neutral for termination. The shipping evidence supports the invariant rows that went 8-for-8: repairs are fast (Abqaiq output largely back within days, per EIA) while traffic through a threatened chokepoint stays depressed long after attacks stop (Suez transits reportedly still 60% below 2023 after about 100 quiet days; secondary source).

## 6. The hike that did not derate equities

The literature says this should not have been a confident row. Kuttner (2001) shows only the unanticipated part of a move affects rates. Bernanke and Kuttner (2005) put the equity response at about 1% for a 25bp surprise, with most announcement-day variance unrelated to policy (the latter recalled). Jarociński and Karadi (2020) find roughly a third of FOMC announcements produce the "wrong" sign, stocks and yields up together; Bauer and Swanson (2023) dispute the information-effect explanation but still estimate only about 2% for a 25bp pure surprise, on the day. Cieslak and Pflueger (2023) note supply-driven and demand-driven inflation are priced differently, and a hike into supply-shock inflation can lower the inflation risk premium, offsetting the higher discount rate.

So 11a's cap of 0.55 on hike-to-equity rows is right, and the rule can be tightened: estimate the surprise component from the futures-implied probability the day before (if the hike was 80% priced, there was little shock left); state the equity leg only for the announcement window, not for weeks; keep yields and mortgage rates as the high-confidence legs, with the caveat that the mortgage spread over the 10-year is itself state-dependent and widens with rate volatility (Brookings). For oil into CPI, the usable rule of thumb is about 0.4 points of headline per 10% oil move, building over roughly a year and fading within two (Choi et al. 2018; Känzig 2021), with near-zero pass-through to core (Fed note, 2017), which supports 13g.

On oil levels, Alquist and Kilian (2010) found futures less accurate than the no-change forecast; later work (Ellwanger and Snudden 2023) finds futures help at longer horizons. No-change is the right default for the horizons the engine uses, which is what range_odds already assumes.

## 7. Market prices as priors and as the benchmark

Read access is free and keyless for Polymarket (gamma-api.polymarket.com for discovery, clob.polymarket.com for prices; both answered without authentication in testing) and for Kalshi (external-api.kalshi.com/trade-api/v2, e.g. markets?series_ticker=KXFED). Manifold is free but play money. Metaculus restricted its API in March 2026, requires a token, and returns community predictions for only a small number of questions on standard accounts (details from a secondary source), so it is not a practical bulk baseline. Polymarket's price-history endpoint has conflicting documentation on authentication and was not tested. Reading data is one thing and trading another; trading on some venues is restricted for US persons.

How far to trust them: Diercks, Katz and Wright (Fed working paper and NBER w34702, 2026) find Kalshi's Fed-decision forecasts at least as good as fed funds futures and its CPI forecasts comparable to the Bloomberg consensus, with thin, stale tail strikes as the stated weakness. Bürgi, Deng and Whelan find Kalshi broadly calibrated with a favourite-longshot bias (contracts at 5 cents win about 2% of the time). A live pull during this research showed a far-dated Fed strike quoted 0.01 bid, 0.99 ask, zero volume, a midpoint of 0.50 that means nothing, so filter on spread, volume and open interest before using any price. On geopolitics, the Swift Centre's professional forecasters sat well below Polymarket on dramatic-change-by-deadline questions (Ukraine ceasefire 22% against 43%); that is anecdote, not a scored study, but it matches the structural reasons longshot "yes" prices float high.

There is no free CME FedWatch API (end-of-day is a paid product). The free routes are the Kalshi ladder, the Atlanta Fed's Market Probability Tracker (downloadable, built from SOFR options, risk-neutral and about the three-month average rate rather than a single meeting), and computing it from fed funds futures: implied average rate = 100 minus price; for a meeting month with N days before and M after, end rate = [average x (N+M) - N x start] / M; probability of a 25bp move = (end - start) / 0.25.

For LLM-assisted forecasting specifically, the recurring result is that the machine adds most when blended with the market, not when substituted for it: Halawi et al. (2024) scored 0.179 against the crowd's 0.149 but a 4:1 crowd-weighted blend beat the crowd alone; the AIA Forecaster lost to liquid markets alone and beat them in ensemble; ForecastBench reports models given market prices simply copy them (correlation 0.994). A sensible rule for this engine, offered as a suggestion since no source gives a number: a written reason is required for any divergence above about ten points from a liquid market, and the edge score is tracked separately for rows that diverge and rows that do not.

## 8. range_odds

Reviews of volatility forecasting (Poon and Granger 2003) most often find option-implied volatility beats historical methods, with GARCH and simple historical estimates roughly tied behind it; Brownlees, Engle and Kelly (2012) find rankings stable from a day to a month and favour long estimation windows. No study was found that scores simple models on range or touch probabilities at one to eight weeks, so the engine's own ledger is the only test.

Free implied-volatility series exist on FRED and as Cboe CSVs: VIXCLS, VXVCLS (three-month), VXNCLS, OVXCLS (oil, 52.11 on 9/17), GVZCLS (gold). Improvements in order of value: use the implied index matched to the horizon where one exists (VIX9D, VIX, VIX3M), with a haircut, since implied runs above subsequent realized volatility on average and oil and gold indices are computed from ETF options; include the -½σ² term so the expected price rather than the expected log price is driftless; otherwise use a mean-reverting estimate (GARCH, or an EWMA blended with long-run volatility) rather than a short window, because after a spike a flat estimate overstates volatility four to eight weeks out, which is exactly the present oil situation; bootstrap standardized residuals for fat tails, which matter most for touch rows; check barriers at every step, and remember daily-close monitoring touches less often than intraday (the usual correction moves the barrier out by exp(0.5826 σ √Δt); recalled).

For data, FRED is the most reliable free source (DCOILBRENTEU, DGS2, DGS10, MORTGAGE30US and the volatility series; oil lags about a week). The EIA API is free with a key. yfinance works but breaks periodically, so cache and never rely on it alone. Stooq now needs an API key (secondary source).

## 9. The LLM pipeline

Anthropic's own guidance distinguishes workflows (fixed code paths) from agents (the model directs itself) and recommends the simplest thing that works. Ingest, triage, analyse, propose is a workflow. This agrees with TypeSafe's guidance and answers the open design question: code should walk each event through fixed steps and call the model for narrow typed judgements, rather than giving one long headless session eighty turns. Atomic yes/no Jev questions composed in code are the same idea at the triage layer.

What the Metaculus bot tournaments found worked: the strength of the base model matters more than scaffolding; aggregating several independent runs helped more than anything else in the scaffold (ten of eleven Q1 2025 prize-winners took a median or mean; four to ten members was the sweet spot in 2026); the Q2 2025 winner wrote separate outside-view and inside-view research notes before forecasting; breadth of research sources correlated with score (r = 0.42); simpler bots beat complex ones (several of these from a secondary digest). Halawi's ablations agree that retrieval is the component that matters (0.206 without it, 0.186 with). Schoenegger et al. (2025) tested 38 prompts: most did nothing, base-rate references helped slightly, and instructing the model to reason in explicitly Bayesian steps hurt. So: sample each proposed probability five or so times independently and take the median, record the spread as an uncertainty signal, keep the outside-view step separate from the narrative step, and do not invest further in prompt wording.

Known failure modes to guard against: hedging toward 0.5 and a bias toward "yes" (Halawi; Schoenegger et al. 2024), recency and rumour overweighting when news is added (Karkar and Chopra 2025, small study), and incoherence. Paleka et al. (ICLR 2025) show consistency violations (negation, paraphrase, conditional checks) correlate with eventual Brier as strongly as 0.9, but that repairing the violations does not improve accuracy. Coherence checks are therefore a cheap early-warning monitor for a book too small to score: P(A) plus P(not A), the timeline siblings summing to one, and contradictory pairs such as #26 against #32 can all be checked in code today.

On security, the design already matches the recommended pattern: untrusted text cannot write the ledger, protected files are hashed and restored, a human approves. Willison's "lethal trifecta" (private data, untrusted content, external communication) is the test to keep applying; the run strips API keys and denies reads of .env, which removes the first leg. The remaining exposure is that web search gives untrusted text a second entry and an outbound channel, so its results deserve the same suspicion as the inbox. CaMeL (2025) formalizes the principle that untrusted data must never alter control flow, which is another argument for code-driven steps.

For Jev, the routing literature (FrugalGPT, RouteLLM) supports a cheap model in front of an expensive one but says nothing about news triage. The validated way to measure a discard stage comes from e-discovery: the elusion test, a random sample of the discarded pile reviewed by the stronger judge to estimate what was missed. Add that (say twenty discarded items per run) alongside the existing agreement log, and track recall against items later cited in approved rows. None of this can start until Jev has answered one live request.

For regression safety, Anthropic's eval guidance suggests twenty to fifty cases drawn from real failures. This engine has them: the eight leg-extrapolation misses, the compound rows, #33's unnoticed resolution, the missed 9/16 hike. Frozen as fixtures with the news available at the time, they test whether a skill patch fixes the old error without breaking the rows that hit. Backtests beyond that are unreliable: date-restricted search still leaks (Paleka et al. 2025; AIA measured 1.65% of results leaking), so live scoring is the only clean test.

## 10. Question writing

Metaculus's guidance is the best available: the clairvoyance test (someone who knows the future could settle the row without asking what was meant), a named resolution source and a fallback, exact dates with timezone, edge cases in fine print, and a clear distinction between "ambiguous" (reality unclear) and "annulled" (the question failed), neither scored. The lint covers wording shapes; it does not yet require a resolution source. Adding source and fallback fields, and writing the intended reading at logging time, would have prevented the #30 problem.

## 11. Build order

| # | Change | Why | Size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reference forecast per row (market price or base rate + class) and edge score with cluster-bootstrap interval in `report` | Gives the scoreboard meaning; every other fix is judged by it | Small |
| 2 | Revisions table, time-weighted scoring, daily price/touch check | Best-evidenced accuracy habit; ends unnoticed resolutions | Medium |
| 3 | Iran rows re-expressed as duration, level and marker rows; next-leg node priced from the cycle's own durations | Matches what the conflict literature finds forecastable | Skill patch |
| 4 | Market fetcher (Kalshi, Polymarket, FRED volatility series) with liquidity filters | Feeds 1 and 8; free | Medium |
| 5 | Coherence monitor: complements, sibling sums, contradictory pairs | Early warning that does not wait for resolutions | Small |
| 6 | Median of about five independent samples per proposed probability; separate outside-view step | Most consistent scaffold gain in bot tournaments | Small to medium |
| 7 | Code-driven per-event workflow with narrow typed steps; atomic Jev questions; elusion sample | Reliability, cost, injection surface | Large |
| 8 | range_odds: implied or mean-reverting volatility, drift term, fat tails, per-step barrier | Better no-view odds, most for touch rows and post-spike oil | Medium |
| 9 | Resolution source, fallback and intended reading as required fields | Ends disputes like #30 | Small |
| 10 | Frozen regression set from past failures | Tests skill patches before they ship | Medium |
| 11 | More short, independent rows outside the Iran cluster | Only route to a sample that can show skill | Habit |

What not to do: fit recalibration curves or extremize on 47 rows; read anything into a bucket with fewer than about 40 rows; add more red-team steps; spend time on prompt wording; trust backtests that use date-restricted search; treat a thin market's midpoint as a price.
